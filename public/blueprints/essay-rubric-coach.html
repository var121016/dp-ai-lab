Foundations
What feedback actually is

Before you write a single line of code, you need a clear idea of what makes feedback useful versus useless. The difference determines everything about how you'll write your prompt.

Think about the last time someone gave you feedback on a piece of writing. What made it helpful? Most students, if they're honest, say: "When it told me exactly what was wrong and why." Not "be more analytical." Not "good structure." Something specific.

✕ Useless feedback
"Your analysis could be deeper. Try to engage more critically with your sources and develop your argument further. The structure is mostly good but the conclusion needs more work."

— This could apply to any essay, by any student, on any topic. Zero diagnostic value.
✓ Useful feedback
"Criterion D (Use of sources): You quote Kennan twice but never explain why his perspective is significant or limited. An examiner needs to see you questioning the source — when was it written, what was Kennan's position, what might he have omitted?"

— Specific, actionable, criterion-linked. The student knows exactly what to do.

The IB rubric is actually a gift here. It tells you exactly what examiners are looking for — criterion by criterion, level descriptor by level descriptor. Your job is to build something that applies those criteria to a student's actual work, not just waves at the essay in general.

The core design principle
Your coach must do two things that generic AI doesn't: (1) know which rubric applies to this specific essay type, and (2) point at the specific sentence or paragraph that needs work — not the essay in general.

There's also a critical ethical line here that the tool must enforce: it gives feedback, it never rewrites. The moment it starts offering to "improve" a sentence, students will use it as a writing tool rather than a feedback tool. Your prompt must make this impossible — not just discouraged.

The line you must build in
If a student asks "can you rewrite this paragraph?", the coach should decline and instead ask: "What did you mean to say in this paragraph? Tell me the idea and I'll help you express it more clearly yourself."

This is what your coach needs to catch — not generic weaknesses, your specific ones.

02 / 05
The Rubric
Know your rubric — deeply

The IB rubric is the only thing that matters to an examiner. Your coach needs to know it at least as well as you do. This part is about understanding the rubric well enough to put it into a prompt.

Different essay types in the DP have very different rubrics. Here's an example — History HL Paper 2. Notice how specific each criterion is. This is what you'll be loading into your system prompt:

Criterion Max What the examiner is actually looking for
A — Thesis 3 A clear, defensible claim that directly answers the question. Not a description of what will be discussed — an actual position.
B — Evidence 6 Accurate, relevant, specific historical evidence. Named people, dates, events, statistics. "Many people died" = 0. "Approximately 6 million Jews were killed in the Holocaust" = evidence.
C — Analysis & argument 6 Does the evidence support the thesis? Does the student explain HOW it supports it, not just state that it does?
D — Source evaluation 5 Are sources used critically? Origin, purpose, value, limitation — not just "this source says X".
Where to find the actual rubric
Your teacher has the IB Subject Guide — ask for the assessment criteria section. You can also find them in past paper markschemes on Follett Shelf or your school's IB resource portal. Copy the exact wording — level descriptors vary by subject and you want the real ones, not a summary.

The most important thing to notice: each criterion has level descriptors — what a Level 1 looks like versus a Level 4. Your prompt needs to include those descriptors so the AI can locate the student's work within the band, not just say "this could be better."

A key decision to make now
Will you build for one subject only, or multiple? Starting with one subject (the one you actually write essays for) is much better. A coach that deeply knows the History HL rubric is more useful than one that half-knows five rubrics. You can add subjects later.
✍ Your turn Write out your rubric in plain English

Pick your subject and essay type. Write out each criterion: the name, the max marks, and in your own words what the examiner is actually looking for. Don't copy the IB jargon — translate it into what it means in practice. This becomes the core of your system prompt.

03 / 05
The Prompt
Design the system prompt

The system prompt is where the rubric becomes a coach. This is the most important part of the entire project — get this right and the rest is just interface. Get this wrong and no amount of polish will fix it.

Here's a worked example — a system prompt for a History HL Paper 2 essay coach. Read it carefully before writing your own. Notice what it does and doesn't allow:

prompts/history-hl.mdSystem prompt
# Essay Rubric Coach — History HL Paper 2

## Who you are

You are an experienced IB History examiner giving feedback on
a student's Paper 2 essay. You have marked hundreds of these essays.
You are direct, specific, and honest. You do not flatter.

## Your method

When given an essay, you:
1. State the essay question (or ask the student to provide it
   if they haven't).
2. Go through each criterion below, one at a time.
3. For each criterion: quote the specific sentence or paragraph
   you are commenting on, then explain what an examiner would
   think reading it, then identify one concrete thing to improve.
4. End with a single priority: the ONE thing that would most
   improve this essay's mark if fixed.

## The criteria (History HL Paper 2)

Criterion A — Thesis (max 3 marks)
A strong thesis: makes a clear, defensible claim; directly answers
the question; takes a position (not just "there were many factors").
Weak thesis: restates the question, describes what the essay will do,
or makes a claim too vague to test against evidence.

Criterion B — Evidence (max 6 marks)
Strong: specific named events, people, statistics, dates, places.
Weak: generalisations ("many people suffered"), vague references
("according to a historian"), or inaccurate facts.
For each paragraph: is there at least one specific piece of evidence?

Criterion C — Analysis & argument (max 6 marks)
Strong: the student explains HOW the evidence supports the thesis —
not just that it does. The argument builds across paragraphs.
Weak: paragraphs that describe events without connecting them to
the thesis; evidence that "stands alone" without interpretation.

Criterion D — Source evaluation (max 5 marks)
Strong: named sources with origin, purpose, and at least one
limitation or value noted. The evaluation is specific to that source.
Weak: "this source is useful because it gives information", or
no engagement with where the source comes from or why that matters.

## What you never do

- Rewrite any part of the essay. If asked, say: "Tell me what you
  were trying to say and I'll help you say it yourself."
- Give a score — you give feedback, not a grade.
- Give vague feedback like "improve your analysis." Every comment
  must point to a specific sentence, quotation, or section.
- Say the essay is good when it isn't. Be honest.

Notice the structure: who the AI is, a clear method to follow, the rubric with level guidance baked in, and explicit "never do" rules. The method section is crucial — it forces the AI to go criterion by criterion rather than giving a general impression.

The most important line in any coaching prompt
"Quote the specific sentence or paragraph you are commenting on." Without this, AI feedback floats above the essay. With it, every comment is anchored to actual text the student can find and change.

One more pattern worth building in: the priority at the end. After going through all criteria, the AI names the single most important thing to fix. This stops students from being overwhelmed by a list of 8 improvements — they know where to start.

✍ Your turn Write your system prompt

Using the structure above and the rubric you wrote in Part 2, write the full system prompt for your essay coach. Keep the "who you are" and "method" sections short — the rubric section should be the longest part. Most importantly: write the "never do" rules for your specific subject. What's the thing students most want the AI to do for them that you want to prevent?

The Build
Build the interface

The interface for this project is deliberately simple — a textarea for the essay, a button, and a results panel. Complexity here is a distraction. The value is in the prompt, not the UI.

You need four things on the page:

1
Subject selector
A dropdown with your supported essay types. Each option loads a different system prompt. Start with just one — add more later. <select id="subject"> with one <option> per rubric.
2
Essay question input
A short text field for the essay question or title. The AI needs this to check whether the thesis actually answers the question. Without it, Criterion A feedback is impossible.
3
Essay body textarea
A large textarea where the student pastes their draft. Add a character counter — IB essays have word limits and students should know if they're over. Most Paper 2 essays should be 800–1000 words.
4
Feedback panel
Where the response appears. Render it as structured sections — one block per criterion — not as a wall of text. Use the criterion name as a heading so students can navigate directly to the area they want to work on.

Here's the core JavaScript that handles the API call. The key difference from a chatbot is that you're sending the full essay as the user message — not just a short question:

public/app.jsJavaScript
// Load system prompts — one file per subject
const PROMPTS = {
  'history-hl': '',   // filled on load
  'economics-ia': '',
};

async function loadPrompts() {
  for (const key of Object.keys(PROMPTS)) {
    const res = await fetch(`/prompts/${key}.md`);
    PROMPTS[key] = await res.text();
  }
}

async function getFeedback() {
  const subject = document.getElementById('subject').value;
  const question = document.getElementById('question').value.trim();
  const essay = document.getElementById('essay').value.trim();

  if (!essay) { alert('Paste your essay first.'); return; }

  // Build the user message — essay question + essay body
  const userMessage = `Essay question: ${question || 'Not provided'}

Essay:
${essay}`;

  showLoading();

  const res = await fetch('/api/feedback', {
    method: 'POST',
    headers: { 'Content-Type': 'application/json' },
    body: JSON.stringify({
      systemPrompt: PROMPTS[subject],
      userMessage: userMessage
    })
  });

  const data = await res.json();
  showFeedback(data.feedback);
}
Store system prompts as separate .md files
Don't hardcode them in your JavaScript. Keep each rubric in prompts/history-hl.md, prompts/economics-ia.md, etc. This way you can update a rubric without touching any code — just edit the markdown file and redeploy.

05 / 05
Test & Deploy
Test it on a real essay — then ship it

The most important test for this project isn't whether it runs — it's whether the feedback is actually useful. You need to test it on a real essay before you share it with anyone.

Before you call it done, run these three tests:

1
Test with a real essay — ideally one that's already been marked
If you have a returned essay with actual IB marks and comments, paste it in. Does the coach find the same weaknesses your teacher did? Does it miss anything important? This is your calibration test.
2
Test the "never do" rules
After getting feedback, type: "Can you rewrite my introduction?" Does it refuse correctly and redirect you? Type: "Just give me a better thesis." Does it hold the line? If not, go back to your system prompt and make the rules more explicit.
3
Test with a deliberately weak essay
Write or paste a poor essay — one with a vague thesis, no specific evidence, and no source evaluation. Does the coach identify all three problems clearly? Does it still point to specific sentences rather than giving general criticism?

Once it passes those three tests, deploying is the same as any static site with a serverless function for the API proxy:

→
Push to GitHub
git add . && git commit -m "Essay rubric coach — working" && git push
→
Add API key to Netlify environment variables
Site settings → Environment variables → ANTHROPIC_API_KEY. The key stays server-side in the Netlify function — never in your HTML or JS.
→
Share the URL with one other student first
Before sharing widely, give it to one person and watch them use it. What confuses them? What do they try to do that you didn't expect? Fix those things before sharing with your class.
The most important thing after you ship
Use it on your own essays, for real, for two weeks. Track whether your marks actually improve. If they do — why? If they don't — what is the feedback missing? The best version of this tool comes from you actually using it, not from building features nobody needs.
✍ Your turn Write your test plan

Before you build anything, write down: which real essay will you test it on first? What are you expecting it to catch? What would "good enough to share" look like for you — what would the feedback need to do that it currently doesn't? This stops you from building forever and never shipping.
